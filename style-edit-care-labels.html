How to read a care label

The symbols are an international standard, they are not difficult, and misreading them is the most common way good clothes are ruined.

The Styled editorial team · August 6, 2026

Care labels are one of the few genuinely standardised things in clothing. The symbols come from an international system, they mean the same thing in every country that uses them, and there are only five families to learn.

They are also routinely ignored, which is how wool ends up child-sized and dark cotton ends up grey. Worth ten minutes.

The five symbols

  • The tub — washing. A number inside is the maximum temperature in Celsius. Dots mean the same thing (one dot 30°C, two 40°C, three 60°C). A hand in the tub means hand wash only. A bar underneath means a reduced-agitation cycle; two bars mean the gentlest cycle your machine has. A cross through it means do not wash in water at all.
  • The triangle — bleach. Empty means any bleach is permitted. Two diagonal lines inside mean oxygen bleach only, not chlorine. Crossed out means none.
  • The square — drying. A circle inside means tumble drying is allowed, with dots for heat. A crossed-out circle means no tumble drying. Lines inside the square instead of a circle describe natural drying: one vertical line for line drying, one horizontal for drying flat — which for knitwear is not optional.
  • The iron — dots for temperature, one to three, low to high. Crossed out means do not iron. Lines beneath mean no steam.
  • The circle — professional cleaning. A letter inside tells the cleaner which solvent to use; it is information for them, not for you. What matters to you is whether it is crossed out.

Bars mean gentler. Across every symbol, a bar underneath means reduce the intensity, and two bars mean reduce it a lot. If you remember nothing else, remember the bars — they are the part people miss, and they are usually the part that matters.

“Dry clean only” is often a liability decision

There is a real distinction hidden here. “Dry clean” is a recommendation. “Dry clean only” is a constraint. Manufacturers apply the stronger wording generously, because a ruined garment washed against instructions is your problem, and one ruined following them is theirs.

What actually decides it is construction and fibre, not the label wording:

  • Genuinely dry clean only: structured tailoring with canvassing or fusing, anything with a lining that will shrink at a different rate to the shell, leather and suede, most viscose, and pleating that has been heat-set.
  • Usually fine by hand, whatever the label says: unstructured knitwear in wool, cashmere or merino; silk in plain weaves and solid colours.

Wool knitwear is the big one. Most of it is labelled dry clean only and most of it hand-washes perfectly well in cool water with a wool-specific detergent, provided you never wring it and always dry it flat. Wool felts from the combination of heat, moisture and agitation — remove any one of the three and it survives.

The instructions that are not negotiable

Some lines on a label are genuine physical limits, and the failure they prevent is irreversible:

  • Do not tumble dry, on anything knitted. Heat plus tumbling is exactly the felting recipe.
  • Dry flat. A wet knit hung on a hanger stretches under its own weight and does not recover.
  • Wash separately, on the first few washes of anything dark. Excess dye leaves in the early washes whatever the label promises.
  • Do not iron, on synthetics. Polyester and acrylic melt rather than scorch, and a melted patch is permanent.

Wash less

The single largest factor in how long clothes last is not how carefully you wash them but how often. Washing is mechanical abrasion; every cycle removes a little fibre, which is what the lint filter is full of.

Wool and most outerwear need washing far less often than habit suggests — airing overnight handles most of what a wash would have addressed. Denim lasts dramatically longer washed rarely and cold. What genuinely needs frequent washing is what sits against skin all day, which is mostly base layers.

The practical consequence is that a garment’s realistic lifespan — the number that decides its cost per wear — is substantially under your control, and most of that control is exercised at the laundry basket rather than in the shop.
